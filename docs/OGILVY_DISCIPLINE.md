# Ogilvy Discipline — No Parade Team Customs

# PART I — POSITIONING AND COPY

You are writing for a considered purchase. A coach spending $900–1,500 of a booster
club's money reads carefully and is accountable to other people for the decision. Write
for that reader.

## 1. The positioning statement — fixed, do not drift

> **No Parade Team Customs makes custom sublimated kits for clubs, leagues, schools and
> organizations that want a designed identity rather than a logo on a blank — delivered
> with a proof in 48 hours by the person who designs it.**

Every piece of copy must serve that sentence. If a line does not advance what it does,
who it is for, or why it is different, cut the line.

Ogilvy's rule: positioning is the single most important decision, and it is decided
before any copy is written. It is decided. Do not re-open it in a headline.

## 2. What you say matters more than how you say it

The big idea is that competitors decorate inventory and No Parade designs identity. Every
headline, subject line and section either carries that idea or is decoration itself.

Reject any line whose only virtue is that it sounds good.

## 3. Facts, never adjectives

Ogilvy sold a Rolls-Royce with a fact about an electric clock, not with the word
"luxurious." Do the same here. The fact bank below is true and verifiable; use it instead
of praise.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "premium quality" | "The color is dyed into the fibre, not printed on top. It cannot crack or peel." |
| "fast turnaround" | "A proof in your inbox within 48 hours. Kits about three weeks from approval." |
| "great for any team" | "Sizes 2XS to 3XL, so your smallest player is not swimming in it." |
| "affordable" | "$58 a jersey. Ten minimum. Names and numbers included, no per-name charge." |
| "personalized" | "Every player's name and number, printed — not heat-pressed after the fact." |
| "we care about design" | "Your kit is drawn by a person in Bayonne, not selected from a catalogue of layouts." |

**Rule:** every claim must be something the operator can prove on a phone call. If it
cannot be proved, delete it. Never write a claim you would not want a coach to test.

## 4. Headlines

Five times as many people read the headline as the body copy. Treat it as the article.

- Put the promise in the headline, not the mood.
- Include news when there is news ("Fall orders open," "Proofs in 48 hours").
- Specifics outperform abstractions. A number beats an adjective.
- Never write a headline that requires the body copy to make sense.
- Never write a headline that could belong to a competitor. If BSN could run it, it is
  not a headline, it is wallpaper.

Weak: *Design Your Team Identity.*
Better: *Your team's kit, designed by a person, proofed in 48 hours.*

## 5. Long copy sells — where it belongs

The instinct to be brief is wrong for the team-order pages. The more you tell, the more
you sell, on a purchase this considered. A coach wants to know about sizing, minimums,
timelines, what happens if a name is misspelled, and who they will be dealing with.
Answer all of it.

Long copy applies to: the team customs landing page, the process page, FAQ, outreach
emails, and the pre-production approval page.

Short copy applies to: the retail brand world — Seven Nations, faith-led product pages,
the homepage. That register is ceremonial and restrained by design.

**Do not mix them.** Persuasion where a decision is being made; restraint where a brand
is being felt. Ogilvy's own rule was to match the copy to the buying decision, and these
are two different decisions.

## 6. Structure for a long-copy page

1. Headline carrying the promise
2. A single fact that demonstrates the promise (the electric clock)
3. What it is, plainly, in one paragraph
4. The process, numbered, with times attached to each step
5. Prices and minimums, stated openly — never "contact us for a quote"
6. The objections, answered before they are raised: sizing, misspellings, deadlines,
   what happens if they do not like the first proof
7. Proof: photographs of real delivered kits, and testimonials with a real name, team
   and town attached
8. A single clear action

## 7. Testimonials and proof

Ogilvy: testimonials from real customers raise credibility more than any claim the
advertiser makes about itself. Until real ones exist, do not invent, imply, or imitate
them. Write "founding season" honestly instead. When they arrive, use the coach's name,
the team, and the town.

## 8. Story appeal

Every brand needs one concrete, human detail people repeat. Here it is not a device — it
is that the person who designs the kit answers the phone, works from Bayonne, and texts
back at nine at night. Use it plainly. Do not dress it up.

## 9. Never write

- Superlatives without evidence: best, premium, ultimate, world-class, unmatched
- Empty intensifiers: truly, incredibly, absolutely, literally
- Hype punctuation: exclamation points, all-caps shouting in body copy
- "Elevate," "unleash," "game-changer," "level up," "next level," "revolutionize"
- Jokes, winks, or knowing asides. Cleverness that draws attention to the writing is
  the copy performing for an audience, which the brand's own thesis rejects
- Vague benefit language where a number would do
- Anything a competitor could run unchanged

## 10. The test before shipping any line

1. Is it true, and can the operator prove it?
2. Does it advance the positioning statement?
3. Could a competitor run this exact line? If yes, rewrite.
4. Would you want a coach's booster committee to read it aloud in a meeting?
5. Does it respect the reader's intelligence?

If a line fails any of these, it does not ship.

## 11. Ogilvy's own summary, which governs everything above

> "You cannot bore people into buying your product. You can only interest them in buying
> it."

Interest comes from facts, specifics, and honest usefulness — not volume, not cleverness,
and not decoration.

# PART II — IMAGERY AND LAYOUT

Ogilvy's evidence on pictures is as specific as his evidence on words, and it is more
often ignored. Apply it to every selling page.

## 12. Photographs, not renders

Photographs attract more readers, are believed more, and are remembered longer than
illustrations. Illustration looks like advertising; a photograph looks like fact.

- Real garments, real teams, real places. The field in Bayonne, not a studio.
- No arty photography. Dramatic angles, heavy grading, and lighting that shows off the
  photographer reduce readership. If the picture is about the picture, it has failed.
- Generated model mockups are proofs, not photographs. They may appear in the design
  sheet and in customer proofs, always labelled. They may not stand in for product
  photography on a selling page once real garments exist.

## 13. The five images this brand must own

Build these before building anything decorative.

**1. The swatch comparison — the demonstration.**
Four panels of the same fabric, printed in garnet, maroon, burgundy and cardinal. One
variable: the color. Overhead at 90°, diffuse daylight or open shade, no flash, no mixed
lighting, locked exposure and white balance. Mid-grey or bone background — never black,
which crushes the dark reds together and destroys the argument. Panels touching, no gaps:
separation lets the eye forgive the difference, contact makes it undeniable. Close enough
that the weave reads as cloth, not a paint chip. Labels set in type afterward, never
physical tags.

**2. The scratch demonstration.** The print resisting a coin. Makes "dyed into the fibre"
a thing the reader watched rather than a claim they were asked to accept.

**3. The wash test.** One garment after one wash beside the same after forty, identical
light and angle. Fading is the parent's real grievance with spirit wear.

**4. The team in place.** A real roster wearing the kit where they play.

**5. One story-appeal detail.** A proof sheet marked in red pen, a sleeve carrying a class
year, the design table. One human detail people repeat.

## 14. Captions

Twice as many people read captions as read body copy, and almost nobody writes them.
Every photograph carries a caption. Every caption carries the promise and, where natural,
the brand name.

> Bayonne's garnet, beside the three reds most spirit wear ships instead. Same fabric,
> same print, same light. Photographed in Bayonne, NJ.

A photograph published without a caption is wasted readership.

## 15. Turn the demonstration into a lead

Follow the swatch image with a real offer:

> Screens lie about red. Ask us for a swatch card and we will mail you one.

A physical sample request is a qualified lead with an address attached, and it converts
the strongest claim in the business into a conversation.

## 16. Page layout for selling pages

Ogilvy tested layout as rigorously as copy. Follow the findings, not current fashion.

- **Photograph on top, headline beneath the photograph, body copy beneath the headline.**
  Headlines set under the image are read more than headlines set above it.
- **Dark text on a light background.** Reversed type — light text on black — measurably
  reduces readership of body copy. This applies to every selling page, regardless of the
  brand's dark aesthetic elsewhere.
- Serif body type, generous line spacing, comfortable measure. Legibility is a form of
  respect for the reader.
- Subheads every few paragraphs so long copy can be scanned before it is read.
- Price and minimum visible without scrolling or clicking. Never a quote gate.
- FAQ answers set as body copy, not hidden in accordions. Every click costs readers.
- One call to action, repeated down the page in the same words.

## 17. Two visual registers — do not mix them

**Brand world** — homepage, Seven Nations, faith-led product. Dark, restrained,
ceremonial. This is where the brand is felt.

**Selling pages** — team customs, program stores, approval pages, outreach. Light,
legible, photographic, long. This is where a decision is made and money is committed.

The ceremonial aesthetic is correct for the brand and wrong for the sale. A reader who
cannot see the product, or cannot read the copy, does not buy it. Match the treatment to
the decision being made, exactly as with the two copy registers.

## 18. The image test before publishing

1. Can a reader tell exactly what they would receive?
2. Does it the picture prove something, or merely decorate?
3. Is it a photograph of a real thing, or a render presented as one?
4. Does it carry a caption that earns its space?
5. Would a competitor's stock library contain something similar? If yes, reshoot.
